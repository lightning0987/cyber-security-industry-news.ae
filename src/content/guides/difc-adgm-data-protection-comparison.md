## Why three regimes exist at once

The United Arab Emirates has a federal data protection law and two financial free zones that
operate their own. This is not an oversight. DIFC and ADGM are common-law jurisdictions with
independent legislative authority, established so that international financial firms could
operate under a familiar legal framework.

The consequence for a data protection programme is concrete. Where your entity is registered
determines which law applies, which regulator supervises you, and which penalty regime you face.

## Which regime applies where

An entity registered in the Dubai International Financial Centre sits under DIFC Data Protection
Law No. 5 of 2020. Its supervisor is the DIFC Commissioner of Data Protection.

An entity registered in Abu Dhabi Global Market sits under the ADGM Data Protection Regulations
2021. Those regulations came into force on 14 February 2021, replacing the 2015 regulations.
The supervisor is the ADGM Office of Data Protection.

An entity registered anywhere else in the United Arab Emirates sits under the federal Personal
Data Protection Law, supervised by the UAE Data Office.

Registration is the test, not physical location and not where customers are. An entity registered in Dubai but outside the DIFC perimeter does not gain DIFC status by
serving DIFC clients.

## What the two free zone regimes have in common

Both are modelled on the European General Data Protection Regulation. ADGM acknowledged the
influence of the DIFC law of 2020 when drafting its own update, and both took GDPR as the
benchmark.

That shared origin produces substantive overlap. Both recognise the same lawful bases for
processing: consent, contract, legal obligation, public interest, legitimate interest and vital
interest. Both require consent to be explicit, informed, freely given and withdrawable.

Both give data subjects a comparable set of rights, both require a data protection officer in
defined circumstances, and both require that personal data transferred abroad receives an
adequate level of protection.

Both also set breach notification obligations that run without undue delay, with 72 hours as the
outer bound familiar from GDPR.

## Where the two regimes differ

The clearest practical difference is enforcement exposure.

The DIFC Commissioner of Data Protection can issue administrative fines, and the maximum
administrative fine under the DIFC regime is one hundred thousand US dollars. The ADGM
regulations carry a higher ceiling.

Beyond penalties, the differences are procedural rather than conceptual. Each zone maintains its
own registration, notification and transfer mechanisms, and each publishes its own guidance.
A control set designed for one will map onto the other, but the filings will not.

### The trap for groups

A group with entities in more than one jurisdiction does not get to pick. A DIFC entity, an ADGM
entity and a mainland entity within the same group answer to three different supervisors under
three different texts.

That means three notification paths and three sets of records, even where the underlying
security controls are shared infrastructure. Consolidating the controls is sensible.
Consolidating the compliance obligations is not available.

## How this interacts with the security regimes

Data protection and information security obligations run in parallel rather than in sequence.

A DIFC-authorised firm sits under the DIFC data protection regime and, if it is regulated by the
DFSA, under DFSA Technology and Risk Management rules as well. Those are separate obligations
with separate evidence.

A Dubai government supplier may sit under DESC ISR v3 and its 13 control domains through
contract, while its personal data obligations run under whichever data protection regime matches
its registration.

An operator of critical national infrastructure sits under the UAE Information Assurance Standard
and its 188 controls regardless of where it is registered, because that standard is federal.

## What evidence each regime expects

All three data protection regimes expect appropriate technical and organisational measures, and
all three judge appropriateness after an incident rather than before.

Penetration testing produces dated, scoped evidence that the technical measures protecting
personal data were tested and that findings were closed. That record supports the
appropriateness argument under any of the three regimes, because the standard of proof is the
same even where the texts differ.

What testing does not do is satisfy the procedural obligations. Records of processing,
notification templates, transfer assessments and officer appointments are separate artefacts
under each regime.

## What the ransomware data adds

Financial Services carries 11 claimed organisations on this tracker, fourth of 13 sectors. The
sector draws 8 distinct ransomware groups, and no single operation dominates it.

That matters for free zone firms specifically. A defence built around the methods of one group
covers a small fraction of the observed activity, while the notification obligations triggered by
any successful incident are identical regardless of which group was responsible.

## Practical sequence

1. List every group entity and record which jurisdiction each is registered in, because the
   registration determines the regime.
2. Identify the supervisor for each entity, since notification goes to different offices.
3. Build one control set across the group, because the security requirements converge.
4. Build separate notification paths per regime, because the procedural requirements do not.
5. Check whether DFSA Technology and Risk Management rules apply on top of the data protection
   regime for regulated firms.
6. Keep dated testing evidence, since every regime judges the appropriateness of measures after
   the fact.
