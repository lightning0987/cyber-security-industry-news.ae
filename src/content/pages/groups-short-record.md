## A single claim describes an episode rather than an actor

Of the 39 groups below the threshold, 24 were recorded against a UAE organisation exactly once.
One claim fixes a date and a sector. It cannot show whether a group returned, changed sector or
stopped operating.

Leak-site monitoring treats each claim as an assertion by the attacker. A single assertion carries
no pattern to read, so this site does not build a profile page on one.

## Fifteen groups sit between the single claim and the threshold

The table above lists the 15 groups with repeat claims that still fall short of 5. Their first and
most recent dates are shown because the gap between them is the useful figure.

Two claims a week apart and two claims two years apart describe different things. The first reads
as one campaign. The second suggests a group that returned to the country after a long absence.

## What a long tail means for defence planning

The practical consequence is that actor names are a weak basis for planning in the UAE.

1. Treat the named groups as the visible part of the record, not as the whole of it.
2. Expect an unfamiliar name, because groups below the threshold hold 54.6% of attributed claims.
3. Plan around sectors and control domains, which persist while group names turn over.
4. Read the [sector pages](/uae-ransomware-tracker/) before the group pages when deciding where to
   spend.
5. Check the [methodology](/methodology/) before citing any of these counts, because the
   attribution rule decides which claims enter them.

The 7 groups with a sustained record are the exception in this dataset, not the rule. A control
that only answers those 7 leaves the larger share of the record unaddressed.
